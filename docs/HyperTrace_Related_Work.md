# HyperTrace Related Work

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Human Review of Coordinated Information Operations

Recent CIO research treats coordination as an operation-level pattern distributed across accounts, relations, content, and time. Cima et al. compared behavioral signals across information operations, while Graham et al. systematized the construction and analysis of coordination networks (Cima et al., 2024; Graham et al., 2024). Other studies show that the same analytical perspective applies to fake-follower campaigns, socio-linguistic account patterns, and manufactured attention that may combine human and automated participation (Zouzou and Varol, 2024; Burghardt et al., 2024; Rogers and Righetti, 2025). Platform affordances also matter: recent work on TikTok demonstrates that video-first interaction changes both the observable signals and the limits of text-centered detection (Luceri et al., 2026). Meanwhile, LLM-based social agents can generate varied content, learn from interaction, and adapt influence strategies. These capabilities reinforce the need to assess suspected coordination through operation-level relational and temporal evidence rather than through automation signals alone (Piao et al., 2025; Jin and Guo, 2025).

These studies establish the need for relational and platform-sensitive detection, but their main outputs remain discovered networks, ranked accounts, or operation labels. They provide limited guidance for a reviewer who must determine whether an alert is justified, whether its evidence existed at the decision cutoff, and whether every displayed claim can be checked against a source record. HyperTrace therefore builds on operation-level detection without treating coordination as proof of wrongdoing. It addresses the downstream review problem: reconstructing the evidential basis of a potentially fallible model assessment so that a reviewer can independently accept, qualify, or reject it.

### 2.2 Explanations and Appropriate Reliance in Human--AI Decisions

Recent human--AI research increasingly evaluates explanations by how they shape reliance rather than by whether users report greater trust. Appropriate reliance requires people to accept well-founded advice while resisting erroneous advice, and it can be affected by explanation content, prior intuition, and perceived fairness (Schemmer et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Schoeffer et al., 2024). Imperfect explanations can make incorrect recommendations more persuasive, showing that explanatory detail is not inherently beneficial (Morrison et al., 2024). More recent cognitive-forcing designs deliberately withhold or stage information to encourage independent reasoning, but their effects remain dependent on task and presentation (de Jong et al., 2025). Together, these findings support measuring final decision accuracy, correct-advice uptake, erroneous-advice rejection, decision time, and workload instead of using trust or agreement as the success criterion.

Most of this evidence comes from decisions with individual predictions or compact feature explanations. CIO review involves a different evidential object: a time-evolving heterogeneous network whose meaning depends on relations, sequence, and source records. A reviewer must inspect not only why a model produced a score, but also whether the displayed mechanism is temporally valid and independently verifiable. HyperTrace extends appropriate-reliance research to this setting by presenting the model output as a preliminary assessment and making evidence inspection, source verification, and rejection of the recommendation explicit parts of the workflow.

### 2.3 Graph Explanations and Hyperbolic Structure

Foundational graph explainers introduced sparse node, edge, or feature masks that preserve a target prediction (Ying et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2020). Recent work has broadened this formulation. Adversarial Mask Explainer studies robustness under perturbation, while game-theoretic counterfactual methods search for actionable graph changes (Zhang et al., 2024; Chhablani et al., 2024). DyExplainer integrates sparse attention into dynamic graph models, GnnXemplar produces global rule-like accounts, curvature-based methods target robust structural explanations, and GNN-EGG generates explanatory graphs (Wang et al., 2025; Armgaan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Taychameekiatchai et al., 2026). These methods improve the scope, robustness, or usability of graph explanations, yet their central criterion is still commonly prediction behavior. A sparse explanation can preserve a logit while altering the relational or geometric organization through which the detector represents coordination.

Recent hyperbolic graph research provides stronger tools for representing such organization. The Lorentz/hyperboloid model and its geodesic distance are standard constructions (Nickel and Kiela, 2018). Hypformer performs Transformer computation in hyperbolic space, and recent heterogeneous models distribute relation types across one or multiple hyperbolic spaces (Yang et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024a, 2024b). SAHG further uses sector-anisotropic hyperbolic representations to distinguish account- and neighborhood-level evidence in social-bot detection (Lu et al., 2026, preprint). However, these models primarily evaluate representation and prediction, whereas graph explainers rarely test whether selected evidence preserves a detector's hyperbolic decision geometry. Neither prediction fidelity nor a visually plausible subgraph guarantees preservation of prototype distances, radial organization, temporal availability, or source-level provenance. HyperTrace addresses this intersection by treating Lorentz geometry as an auditable property of cutoff-safe evidence reconstruction, not as a claim that hyperbolic models universally outperform Euclidean ones.
